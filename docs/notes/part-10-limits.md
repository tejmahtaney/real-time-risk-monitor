# Part 10 - Limits and alerts

## Key maths

For a limit with configured ceiling `L` and a current reading `value` (already transformed to a non-negative "how much of the bad thing is there" figure — see below), utilisation is `u = |value| / L`. The raw state from `u` alone is `Breach` at `u ≥ 1`, `Warning` at `u ≥ warning_threshold`, else `Ok`. Hysteresis overrides that raw state in two symmetric cases:

- If the *previous* state was `Breach` and `u ≥ clear_threshold`, the state stays `Breach`.
- If the *previous* state was `Warning` or `Breach` and `u ≥ warning_clear_threshold`, the state stays at least `Warning` (a `Breach` that has fallen under `clear_threshold` lands at `Warning`, not `Ok`, unless `u` has *also* fallen under `warning_clear_threshold`).

`warning_clear_threshold < warning_threshold < clear_threshold < 1`, so the entry line for a state is always higher than the line that clears it — a value sitting exactly on either boundary cannot flicker on rounding noise; it has to retreat by a real margin first. `next_state` (`crates/risk/src/monitor.rs`) implements this as: a limit at `Ok` needs to reach `warning_threshold` to enter `Warning`; a limit already at `Warning` or `Breach` needs to fall under the lower `warning_clear_threshold` to reach `Ok`.

The theta floor is the one limit whose `value` is not the raw risk figure. Portfolio theta is usually negative (time decay costs a book that is net long optionality), so the floor is compared against `max(0, -theta)`: the day's decay cost, zero whenever theta is flat or positive. A book that is *earning* theta (net short optionality) can never breach this limit, which is the point — it is a floor on how much the book pays, not a two-sided band on theta itself.

## What each limit means to a risk manager

- **Portfolio |dollar delta|** — how much the book's value moves for a 1 % move in every underlying together. The single number a manager glances at first: "are we long or short the market, and by how much."
- **Portfolio |dollar gamma 1 %|** — how fast that delta itself would move in a 1 % shock. High gamma means the hedge you put on this morning is already wrong by lunchtime; it is the cost of staying delta-hedged in a fast market.
- **Portfolio |vega|** — dollars of P&L per vol point. Exposure to a repricing of risk itself (an implied-vol shock), independent of any move in the underlyings.
- **Portfolio theta floor** — the daily rent the book pays for its optionality. A manager who is comfortable losing $2,000 a day to time decay in exchange for long-gamma protection wants to know the moment that rent goes to $5,000.
- **Gross exposure** — Σ|dollar delta| over underlyings, ignoring offsets between them. Two positions that hedge each other in net delta still both carry real notional and real tail risk; gross exposure is the number that does not let them hide each other.
- **Concentration** — the largest single underlying's share of gross exposure. A book can be "diversified" by position count and still be one bad SPY morning away from ruin; this catches that.
- **Per-underlying |dollar delta|** — the same directional check as the portfolio limit, but per name, so one very large single-stock bet cannot hide inside an otherwise-flat portfolio total.

## Why hysteresis matters — and why it needs two lines, not one

Without it, a value sitting almost exactly on a threshold would flip back and forth on every tick of ordinary market noise, and a downstream alerting or paging system would treat each flip as a fresh incident. A single `clear_threshold` around the breach line (Part 10's first cut) fixes that line, but leaves the warning/Ok line exposed. In practice that is not a minor gap: this simulator's risk figures are continuous, driftless random walks, so a value that spends part of a session near the warning line crosses it dozens of times with no protection there, exactly as the tuning story below found the hard way. `warning_clear_threshold` closes that gap the same way `clear_threshold` closes the breach one: once elevated, a limit needs to fall back by a real margin, not just dip below the line that raised the alarm, before it counts as clear.

The two thresholds are not symmetric in one respect worth naming: entering is deliberately easy (reaching `warning_threshold` from `Ok`, or 1 from `Warning`, is instant — the risk is real right now) and leaving is deliberately hard (falling under the *lower* `warning_clear_threshold` or `clear_threshold`). That asymmetry is what "hysteresis" means here: the alert pane should never wonder whether a flicker was real, only whether the retreat was.

## How the demo values were picked (revised)

`cargo run -p engine --example tune_limits` runs 10 headless sessions of seed 42 over the sample portfolio and prints, for every candidate measure, its distribution and (against the current `config/limits.toml`) how many times each session enters and clears `Warning`/`Breach`. The first version of this part's tuning picked `portfolio.dollar_delta = 550,000` specifically because the sample book's static stock (500 SPY, 300 AAPL) already carries about $490,000 of dollar delta at the open — that limit breaches on tick one of every session and, because the book's dollar delta never dips anywhere near `0.95 × 550,000` in any of the 10 sessions, it never clears either. That is a real, honest signal (the book truly is over budget all day), but it means the whole 10-session run produces exactly one alert event, ever, after which the alert pane is static — not what a "1 to 3 alerts a session, with clears" demo needs.

Two changes fixed this:

1. **`warning_clear_threshold` gives the warning line the same hysteresis the breach line already had** (see above), so a threshold that used to flap dozens of times per session because the value lingered right on the line now settles once it clears by a real margin.
2. **The tuning tool got a grid search.** Manually sweeping candidate limits one `cargo run` at a time (as the first pass did) cannot realistically explore a measure's full range against all 10 sessions at once. `Series::search_best_limit` now replays the *exact* state machine (`next_state`, duplicated from `risk::monitor` so the search can run offline against an already-recorded series) against ~200 log-spaced candidate limits per measure, scoring each by how many of the 10 sessions land with 1-3 `Warning`/`Breach` entries and whether any session shows a clear. Run against every candidate measure, it immediately surfaced the one with the best shape: **TSLA's per-underlying |dollar delta|** (the book's bull call spread — long the at-the-money call, short the call 10 % out of the money, both 30 days out) swings from about $48,000 to $111,000 within a single session as spot moves relative to the two strikes, and a limit near $74,000 sits inside that swing cleanly enough to give **every one of the 10 tuning sessions between 1 and 3 entries**, most with at least one clear back to `Ok`.

The final config keeps both ideas: `portfolio.dollar_delta = 550,000` stays as a deliberate, permanent, structural breach (the book really is always over this budget — a real, if static, story worth showing), and `underlying_dollar_delta.overrides.TSLA = 74,000` is the dynamic demo trigger that answers the actual complaint. Combined, `tune_limits` reports per-session `(enters, clears)` of `[(3,1), (1,0), (2,4), (2,2), (1,0), (2,2), (3,3), (1,0), (2,2), (1,1)]` — every session in `[1, 3]` enters, seven of the ten sessions showing at least one clear — with the first alert at simulated `+30s`, 0.2 real seconds into a default-speed run (from the permanent portfolio breach, unchanged from before). Every other limit (dollar gamma, vega, theta floor, gross exposure, concentration, the AAPL/NVDA-covering default, and the MSFT/SPY overrides) is still set safely above its observed 10-session maximum, so it stays quiet in this sample but is a real ceiling a bigger move would still trip.

## Design choices the prompt left open

1. **Wall time comes from an injected `Clock`.** `risk` cannot depend on `marketdata` (`SystemClock` lives there; see CLAUDE.md's dependency direction), so `LimitMonitor::new` takes a `Box<dyn domain::Clock>` — production code passes a real clock, tests and the tuning example pass `FixedClock`. This mirrors `HistoryLoader`'s existing pattern.
2. **`LimitState::Ok` is never stored.** The "active" map only holds entries currently in `Warning` or `Breach`; an absent key means `Ok`. This makes "active alerts" a free iteration over the map rather than a filter, and keeps the hysteresis lookup a single line.
3. **History is one pool of the last 200 events, not 200 per limit.** The prompt's "bounded history of the last 200 events" reads as one shared ring buffer across every limit and scope, which is also what a single alerts pane in the dashboard (Part 15) will want to render.
4. **`AlertEvent`/`LimitStatus` name the limit with a typed `LimitKind` enum, not a string.** Consistent with `StepKind` and `DataMode` elsewhere in the codebase, and it means a typo in a limit name is a compile error, not a silent no-op.
5. **A `Breach` that clears below `clear_threshold` lands at `Warning`, not `Ok`, unless utilisation has *also* fallen under `warning_clear_threshold` in that same tick.** This treats "just cleared a breach" the same as "already at warning" for the purpose of the (lower) clearing line, rather than giving a freshly-cleared breach a free pass straight to `Ok`.
6. **`tune_limits`' grid search duplicates `next_state` instead of exporting it.** The function is a five-line, easily-copied piece of `risk::monitor`'s private state machine; duplicating it in the example keeps `risk`'s public API free of a method that exists only to let an offline analysis script replay history, at the cost of the two copies needing to be kept in step (flagged in both places' doc comments).

## Interview questions

1. **Why does clearing a `Breach` land at `Warning` instead of `Ok`, even though the raw utilisation might already be below `warning_threshold`?** Because "already below `warning_threshold`" is the wrong test once a limit has been elevated — the whole point of `warning_clear_threshold` being lower than `warning_threshold` is that leaving an elevated state should require more room than entering it needed. Using `warning_threshold` as the exit test for a state that started at `Breach` would silently drop the hysteresis it just gained from `clear_threshold`.
2. **Why is the theta floor compared against `max(0, -theta)` instead of `|theta|`?** `|theta|` would also flag a book that is *earning* a lot from time decay, which is not a risk anyone wants an alert for. The floor is specifically about how much the book pays, so only the negative side of theta counts.
3. **The first tuning pass produced a demo with exactly one alert, ever. What was actually wrong, and was the fix a config change or a design change?** Both, and the config change only became possible once the design gap was closed. The immediate symptom (one permanent breach, no clears) was a config problem — the limit was too tight to ever recover. But no config value could have fixed it on its own: any threshold placed *inside* a measure's typical range flaps constantly with no hysteresis at the warning line, and the only threshold that did not flap (comfortably below the book's structural floor) could never clear either. Adding `warning_clear_threshold` — a design change — is what made a *dynamic* threshold (one that both enters and clears within a session) possible at all; the grid search then found where to put it.
