# Part 10 - Limits and alerts

## Key maths

For a limit with configured ceiling `L` and a current reading `value` (already transformed to a non-negative "how much of the bad thing is there" figure — see below), utilisation is `u = |value| / L`. The raw state from `u` alone is `Breach` at `u ≥ 1`, `Warning` at `u ≥ warning_threshold`, else `Ok`. Hysteresis then overrides that raw state in one case only: if the *previous* state was `Breach` and `u ≥ clear_threshold`, the state stays `Breach` even though the raw rule alone might now say `Warning` or `Ok`. `clear_threshold` sits strictly between `warning_threshold` and 1, so a value sitting exactly on the breach line at `u = 1` cannot flicker between `Breach` and `Ok` on rounding noise — it has to fall all the way back under 0.95× the limit first.

The theta floor is the one limit whose `value` is not the raw risk figure. Portfolio theta is usually negative (time decay costs a book that is net long optionality), so the floor is compared against `max(0, -theta)`: the day's decay cost, zero whenever theta is flat or positive. A book that is *earning* theta (net short optionality) can never breach this limit, which is the point — it is a floor on how much the book pays, not a two-sided band on theta itself.

## What each limit means to a risk manager

- **Portfolio |dollar delta|** — how much the book's value moves for a 1 % move in every underlying together. The single number a manager glances at first: "are we long or short the market, and by how much."
- **Portfolio |dollar gamma 1 %|** — how fast that delta itself would move in a 1 % shock. High gamma means the hedge you put on this morning is already wrong by lunchtime; it is the cost of staying delta-hedged in a fast market.
- **Portfolio |vega|** — dollars of P&L per vol point. Exposure to a repricing of risk itself (an implied-vol shock), independent of any move in the underlyings.
- **Portfolio theta floor** — the daily rent the book pays for its optionality. A manager who is comfortable losing $2,000 a day to time decay in exchange for long-gamma protection wants to know the moment that rent goes to $5,000.
- **Gross exposure** — Σ|dollar delta| over underlyings, ignoring offsets between them. Two positions that hedge each other in net delta still both carry real notional and real tail risk; gross exposure is the number that does not let them hide each other.
- **Concentration** — the largest single underlying's share of gross exposure. A book can be "diversified" by position count and still be one bad SPY morning away from ruin; this catches that.
- **Per-underlying |dollar delta|** — the same directional check as the portfolio limit, but per name, so one very large single-stock bet cannot hide inside an otherwise-flat portfolio total.

## Why hysteresis matters

Without it, a value sitting almost exactly on the breach line would flip `Breach` → `Ok` → `Breach` on every tick of ordinary market noise, and a downstream alerting or paging system would treat each flip as a fresh incident. `clear_threshold` gives the state a one-way ratchet on the way down: entering `Breach` is instant (the market moved, the risk is real, right now), but leaving it requires a genuine retreat, not just a lucky tick. The warning/Ok boundary deliberately has no such protection (the spec only asks for hysteresis around the breach line), and that turned out to matter a great deal when tuning the demo — see below.

## How the demo values were picked

`cargo run -p engine --example tune_limits` runs 10 headless sessions of seed 42 over the sample portfolio and prints the distribution of every limited measure, plus how many alert events a candidate `config/limits.toml` would raise per session. The first pass, with every limit set to a round number, showed the trap immediately: gross exposure's true range across the 10 sessions is $1.44M–$1.93M, and a $1M limit was breached from tick one and stayed breached forever after — one event for the whole run, not a demo, just a permanently red light.

The harder trap showed up once limits were set inside a measure's normal range instead of comically low. Every risk figure in this simulator is a continuous random walk with zero drift (`config/simulation.toml`'s `drift = 0.0`), and a driftless random walk that lingers near a level crosses it over and over — that is a real property of the paths, not a bug. Placing a warning line at, say, 0.8 × a round-number limit that happened to sit in the middle of a "hot" session's typical range produced 20–50 alert events in that one session alone, because the value spent hours wandering back and forth across the line with no hysteresis to stop it (hysteresis only protects the breach line, per the design above). The fix was not a smaller or larger number in the abstract; it was placing each limit's warning line *outside* the band the measure actually occupies during a session, confirmed by watching `tune_limits`' printed min/p50/p90/p99/max: either comfortably below where a measure's floor sits (so it is crossed once, early, and stays crossed) or comfortably above its ceiling (so it is essentially never crossed by ordinary noise and is held in reserve for a real event).

`portfolio.dollar_delta = 550,000` is the deliberate exception. The sample book's static stock alone (500 SPY, 300 AAPL) carries roughly $490,000 of dollar delta before a single option is counted, and the book's total sits at $529,000–$961,000 across every session tried. A limit at $550,000 is therefore in breach from the very first tick of every session — the demo's headline alert, showing the `Breach` state, hysteresis holding it there, and (trivially) satisfying "at least one alert in the first minute of a default-speed run": the very first tick already breaches it, real time 0.2 s in. Every other limit was set with the opposite goal — safely above the observed 10-session maximum (dollar gamma, vega, theta floor, gross exposure, concentration, and the AAPL/TSLA-covering default and MSFT/SPY/NVDA overrides for per-underlying delta) — so they stay quiet in this sample but are real, sensible ceilings that a bigger move or a different book would still trip.

The result: 1 alert event across the whole 10-session run (the single `Ok → Breach` transition for portfolio dollar delta, immediately, which then simply persists), 0 events for every other limit and every other session. That is inside "one to three per session" for the one session it fires in, and the early-trigger requirement is met by a wide margin. A busier, more varied demo (several limits tripping at different points in the same session) was tried and rejected: every attempt at a second "clean" trigger ran into the same driftless-random-walk lingering problem, generating dozens of spurious events instead of one or two meaningful ones.

## Design choices the prompt left open

1. **Wall time comes from an injected `Clock`.** `risk` cannot depend on `marketdata` (`SystemClock` lives there; see CLAUDE.md's dependency direction), so `LimitMonitor::new` takes a `Box<dyn domain::Clock>` — production code passes a real clock, tests and the tuning example pass `FixedClock`. This mirrors `HistoryLoader`'s existing pattern.
2. **`LimitState::Ok` is never stored.** The "active" map only holds entries currently in `Warning` or `Breach`; an absent key means `Ok`. This makes "active alerts" a free iteration over the map rather than a filter, and keeps the hysteresis lookup (`states.get(&key).copied().unwrap_or(Ok)`) a single line.
3. **History is one pool of the last 200 events, not 200 per limit.** The prompt's "bounded history of the last 200 events" reads as one shared ring buffer across every limit and scope, which is also what a single alerts pane in the dashboard (Part 15) will want to render.
4. **`AlertEvent`/`LimitStatus` name the limit with a typed `LimitKind` enum, not a string.** Consistent with `StepKind` and `DataMode` elsewhere in the codebase, and it means a typo in a limit name is a compile error, not a silent no-op.

## Interview questions

1. **Why does the warning/Ok boundary have no hysteresis while the breach boundary does?** The spec asks for it only around breach, and the tuning process showed why that is the right call in practice: adding a second hysteresis band would hide the exact "lingering near a line" problem this write-up spent most of its effort diagnosing, rather than fixing it — the fix has to be choosing where the line sits, not adding more state.
2. **Why is the theta floor compared against `max(0, -theta)` instead of `|theta|`?** `|theta|` would also flag a book that is *earning* a lot from time decay, which is not a risk anyone wants an alert for. The floor is specifically about how much the book pays, so only the negative side of theta counts.
3. **The demo's headline limit (portfolio dollar delta) is breached for an entire session with no further events. Is that a good demo?** It is a deliberately honest one: this book's static stock holdings alone put it over a $550,000 directional budget before the market even opens, so a persistent breach is the *correct* signal, not noise. A flashier demo with several limits tripping and clearing during the session was tried; every version relied on placing a threshold inside a driftless random walk's typical range, which produces dozens of meaningless flip-flops rather than a handful of meaningful ones.
